Edmonton gives you a lot of photograph if you are willing to walk. The river cuts the city in two, the bridges do the work of a skyline, and the Legislature grounds still look like a film set when the light is low. I shoot fashion, portraits, and editorial work on those same public places, and I have learned which ones behave and which ones fight you.

This is not a permit manual. Rules change, and a blog is the wrong place to treat them as settled. Before you bring a crew, a light, a large group, or anything that takes over a plaza, read the current notes on the City of Edmonton site for that park or bridge. If a space is closed for an event, we move. I would rather lose a pretty idea than stand in someone else's way.

If you want the fashion side of this city first, the [fashion and editorial](/fashion-editorial/) pages are the pictures. This note is about how I choose the ground under them.

## The Legislature grounds

The Alberta Legislature is the place I return to when a gown needs architecture. The columns, the lamp posts, and the long walks give you lines without a studio. On a clear day the sky goes very blue, which is either a gift or a problem depending on the dress. A black gown under a lamp post can hold that blue. A pale dress can disappear into it, so I watch the sun and move us into the shade of the building when the highlight gets rude.

It is a public building, and it is busy. Tour groups, school visits, and people who simply came to see the grounds are part of the frame until they are not. I do not ask strangers to leave. I wait, I change the angle, or I use a longer lens and let the columns go soft. If you have a train, a veil that needs a wide path, or a crew with stands, that is the moment to check the city's rules before we pick a time. I can work with a small portrait session on foot. I will not pretend a production has the same freedom.

Winter is honest there. The stone stays dark, the snow draws the edges, and breath shows up in the frame if we are not careful. I like that. It looks like Edmonton, not like a rented backdrop.

## Walterdale Bridge

Walterdale is the arch. From the river path you get the steel, the water, and the downtown skyline in one glance, which is why it keeps showing up in my Edmonton pictures. The sun can sit in the steel and flare, and that flare is a choice, not an accident I apologize for. If you want it, we place you so the arch frames you and the flare stays at the edge. If you want a clean face, we turn until the sun is a rim and not a blob.

The bridge is a working piece of the city. Cyclists, runners, and people crossing for an ordinary day will pass through. I build the pose so we can pause when they do, then start again. A fashion frame can survive a passerby. A quiet portrait usually cannot, so I shoot the portrait in the gaps.

Wind on the river is the thing people forget. A gown will travel. Hair will travel. I tell you that before we leave, not after the first frame. If the look depends on a still hem, we pick a pocket of the path that is less open, or we wait for the gust to finish.

## High Level Bridge

High Level is a different shape. I like the underside, where the steel repeats and the sun finds a slot at the end of the day. It feels industrial in a way the Legislature never will, and that is useful. A leather jacket, a red coat, a simple black dress: they all sit well against that repetition. You do not need a ball gown for this bridge to work. You need a shape the steel can argue with.

The path underneath is shared, and the light changes fast once the sun drops behind the structure. I plan High Level as a short, decisive stop, not as the whole afternoon. We arrive with the idea already chosen, we shoot the idea, and we leave before the path goes dim and the tripods of other people fill the same slot. If you want a longer session, we start in the valley and end here, or the other way around, depending on the month.

## The river valley

The valley is the reason the skyline looks the way it does from the far bank. At dusk the towers go pink and the trees go black, and you can stand across the water and let the city be the subject. That frame is less about a face and more about where you live. I use it when a story needs Edmonton itself, not only a person in Edmonton.

Closer in, the paths give you trees, stairs, and long walks that work for couples and for editorial movement. The ground can be icy. The stairs can be slick. I will not put you in a heel on a grade that is not safe, and I will say so out loud. Bring a boot you can walk in, and change into the shoe when we are on flat ground. That one habit saves more pictures than a new dress.

The valley is large enough that "river valley" is not one location. Tell me the mood. Open sky, dense trees, a bridge in the distance, or a quiet path. I will pick the corner that matches, and I will have a second corner ready when the first is full.

## Light, season, and what to bring

Edmonton light is seasonal in a way a studio is not. Summer evenings stay late, which is generous and also a trap, because you think you have more time than the shadows will give you. Winter days are short and blue, and the pretty hour can be over while you are still parking. I book winter sessions earlier than people expect, and I would rather start in the good light than "just grab coffee first."

Smoke season happens. If the sky is grey with it, I will say whether the pictures will still be worth making. Sometimes the low contrast is beautiful. Sometimes it is just flat, and we move the day.

What I ask you to bring is simple. A layer you can shoot in, a layer you can walk in, and a bag that is not the subject unless we want it to be. Phones stay in the bag during the frames. I will show you a few on the back of the camera so you know we are close, then we keep going. The full edit comes later, in a private gallery, which is also how I describe the work on the [services](/services/) page.

## Before you book the spot

Pick the feeling before you pick the pin on a map. Architecture, steel, water, or trees. I can shoot more than one of those in a session if the travel between them is short and the light agrees. I cannot shoot four neighbourhoods and a gown change and still give each picture the time it needs.

Tell me who is in the frame, what you want the pictures for, and whether stairs or long walks are a problem. I work in Edmonton, St. Albert, and Fort Saskatchewan, and I will travel in Alberta when the idea is worth the drive. Pricing stays on request, because a Legislature portrait and a full valley editorial day are not the same job.

If you already know the place, say so when you [book a shoot](/book/). If you only know the mood, that is enough. I will bring the map.
